# Reading and Thinking Through a Paper

Build an account of the paper's problem, approach, and evidence while developing
your own understanding of the problem. Use this sequence for a full reading;
for a targeted check, inspect the relevant passage and the context needed to
interpret it. Record the useful outcome in the existing paper note.

## Orient before reading in detail

Browse the abstract and figures or tables to identify the work's type, research
question, central idea, and main reported result. Note publication and affiliation
information already supported by the source or register. Use it for context;
judge the work through its reasoning and evidence.

Locate the specific research question in the abstract or introduction. Explain
what is being solved, why it matters, and how it relates to your target question.
Keep the authors' formulation distinct from your interpretation.

## Understand the background and form an initial approach

Use the introduction, related work, and compared methods to understand how the
problem arose. What evidence shows that it occurs? Which approaches already
address it, what have they achieved, and what difficulty remains? Retain the
conditions behind those achievements and limitations.

Before studying the proposed method in detail, consider how you would approach
the problem with the stated inputs and constraints. Sketch an operation or line
of reasoning, the information it needs, and why it might help. This is an initial
thought to compare with the paper, not a required research proposal. If the method
is already familiar, reflect honestly from that knowledge rather than claiming
an independent discovery.

## Read the method against the problem

Trace inputs through the main components to outputs. Identify what changed from
prior work, the role of each change, and the assumptions on which it depends.
Ask whether those assumptions hold in the intended setting and whether the
operation addresses the stated difficulty.

Compare the authors' approach with your initial thought: where does each obtain
information, what can each resolve, and what costs or limitations follow? Revise
your understanding when the paper answers an objection. A useful alternative
needs a plausible connection to the problem, not merely a different module name.
Keep a missing capability visible, such as how a proposed checker obtains a
reliable target; naming the checker does not supply that target.

## Examine the support and decide where to go deeper

For experiments, examine why the datasets and baselines were chosen, what the
protocol and metrics measure, and whether the coverage, controls, uncertainty,
and ablations support the conclusion. For theoretical work, trace the claim
through its assumptions and argument. Preserve distinctions such as failure to
establish a claim versus evidence against it.

For a central paper, inspect available code when implementation details affect
your interpretation. Consider reproduction when an unresolved result could
change the research decision, and route execution to the experiment workflow.
Record what was actually inspected or run; released code alone is not evidence
of successful reproduction.

Follow concepts, techniques, or cited methods that are necessary to understand
the argument. Resolve a local definition in context first; route needed new
papers or citation expansion to paper-discovery. When the question concerns how
multiple works fit together or how a topic developed, use research-synthesis.

## Reflect after reading

Leave time to think about the work beyond extracting its contents. Revisit the
problem's relevance, the validity of assumptions, and possible weaknesses in
the method or support. Also consider what you learned, how your initial approach
changed, and whether a different operation could address a remaining difficulty.
Criticism and agreement should both follow the evidence.

Keep worthwhile reflections in the note's analyst section. Separate the source
observation, your interpretation, and an untested proposal; explain why the
proposal might affect the observation and its most consequential unknown.
Check the meaning of a source claim before using it as a premise. Questions and
clarified understanding are useful outcomes even when no new method follows.

Develop complete candidates through research-ideation when that is the requested
task. Reading and reflection require neither an idea count nor a separate
opportunity-mining artifact.
