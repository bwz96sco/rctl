# Routes into a research question

Use the sections relevant to the material and requested perspective. Each route
offers thinking operations, not a fixed sequence, quota or separate report. A
question can combine routes or emerge during ordinary reading and experimentation.

## Failure or bottleneck

Inspect what actually failed, under which information and resource conditions.
Separate a scoring/interface defect, an isolated implementation error and a
potentially recurring obstacle. Establish only the recurrence the evidence supports.
Ask which plausible explanations predict different outcomes: missing information,
inadequate representation, failed reasoning, difficult construction or poor selection
may call for different questions.

Move from the instance to a scoped capability or bottleneck: who encounters it,
what makes it consequential, and what existing approaches already handle. A
counterexample can motivate development before natural prevalence is established;
it does not establish that prevalence. A familiar fix can resolve the instance
without supplying a research contribution. Keep that possibility available.

## Successful effect

Begin with a supported benefit and the conditions under which it was measured.
Develop competing accounts of its source: a new capability, better access to an
existing one, additional resources, selection or a changed evaluation may imply
different conclusions. Ask what each account predicts when the relevant condition
changes, and which prediction would alter a reader's understanding or choices.

An explanation or applicability boundary can be the contribution. A new algorithm
is optional; the next investigation may use existing tools to distinguish accounts.
If the claimed benefit itself is uncertain, keep establishing it separate from
explaining it.

## Conflicting findings

Write down what each source actually claims, with its population, inputs, resources,
endpoint and uncertainty. Align conditions before attributing a disagreement to a
hidden cause. Apparent contradictions can disappear once the claims are comparable.

For a residual disagreement, ask which condition could explain it and what different
answers would imply. The outcome may be a boundary question, a needed comparison
or a resolved discrepancy with no new candidate. A difference between two settings
does not alone establish which changed factor caused it.

## Goal, scenario or assumption change

Start from who needs what capability and why the current task definition might be
inadequate. Identify the assumption that creates the mismatch: complete information,
a single interaction, a static environment or a particular unit of analysis, for
example. Distinguish a real requirement from a convenient inherited convention.

Change an assumption coherently and consider the consequences. Ask what knowledge
or capability the revised setting requires, why that requirement matters, and how
someone could tell whether it is met. Existing data may still be useful while the
task, interaction or evaluation changes. Difficulty alone is not a reason to create
a benchmark. Define the capability before deciding that a benchmark is the needed
research output.

## Measurement or construct ambiguity

Name an important distinction existing observations conflate and the consequence
of that conflation. Consider both relevant changes that should be visible and
irrelevant variation that should not change the judgment. Ask how those judgments
could be established independently of the proposed measure.

Compare what existing measures and simple observations can already distinguish.
The research question may concern construct validity, an information/cost tradeoff,
or a consequential empirical finding using known instruments. Renaming a score is
not sufficient; neither is requiring every measurement study to improve a solver's
terminal accuracy. The operational consequence could be an explanation, prediction
or a better-informed decision, with its own appropriate evidence.

## New method or technical capability

Keep three things distinct: the capability actually measured, the need inferred
from the target setting, and what existing alternatives can already do. A relative
speedup or a small syntax check does not establish a guarantee, a previously
impossible observation or a new research opportunity. Repeated work by users may
motivate a question; its cause and cost still need evidence.

Connect the demonstrated affordance, with its conditions and limits, to an
important obstacle or a potentially useful observation. Compare simpler existing
ways to address the same need; leave an unmeasured feasibility boundary unknown.
Explain what could be learned if the hoped-for application fails as well as what
success might enable. Do not assume either outcome supplies a publishable finding.

Use a cross-domain analogy explicitly: what relation transfers, what differs and
which premise must hold in the target setting? An analogy generates a question,
not evidence that the transfer succeeds. If the user already supplies the target
question and wants designs, use research-ideation directly. Otherwise develop
problem-method fit before selecting an implementation based on popularity.

## Refining across routes

Let a first round expose substantively different questions before comparative
judgment. Useful changes include the object being studied, available information,
time horizon, population, unit of analysis and rival explanation. Apply only changes
that make sense for the inquiry; a Cartesian product of options is not a required
deliverable.

Relate each developed question to a motivating observation or need, the current
state of knowledge and an answer someone would care about. Evidence checks can
resolve a question, change its scope or stimulate another framing. Preserve that
reason rather than accumulating rejected cards. Known methods can be instruments
for a new question, and unknown novelty should remain explicitly unknown.
