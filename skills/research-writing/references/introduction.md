# Introduction

Explain why the paper is needed and what it contributes. Develop the same central argument as the abstract with the definitions, prior evidence, and reasoning a reader needs to understand it. Expand the argument rather than expanding each abstract sentence mechanically.

## Build four connected moves

| Move | Reader's question | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Establish the research area | Why does this problem matter? | Identify the task, its scientific or practical stakes, and essential concepts. Start close enough to the actual problem that the background leads naturally to it. |
| Situate prior work | What is already known or possible? | Select the most relevant approaches, findings, or resources, including the closest work. Explain what they established and how they shape the current question. |
| Identify the gap | Why is another study needed? | State a specific unresolved question, limitation, or scope boundary, and explain its consequence in the setting this paper addresses. |
| Present this work | What does this paper do about it? | State the objective, central idea or study design, supported answer, and concrete contributions. Explain the relevant distinction from prior work. |

The moves describe rhetorical functions, not mandatory paragraph boundaries. Keep prior-work coverage selective here; a separate Related Work section can carry the fuller comparison. Once the gap is clear, introduce the paper's work directly.

## Make the gap specific and supported

Replace a generic claim such as "this problem remains challenging" with the actual unresolved issue. Depending on the evidence, identify a failure setting, an unmet assumption, an unmeasured capability, a prohibitive cost, missing data, an unclear mechanism, or conflicting findings. Explain why that issue matters for the stated task.

Credit prior achievements before explaining their limits. A result outside a previous study's intended scope is a boundary, not automatically a defect. Support literature and novelty claims with the relevant sources; phrases such as "few studies" or "the first" require coverage that justifies them. If coverage is incomplete, narrow the claim or mark the missing review instead of asserting absence.

Connect the proposed answer to this precise gap. Distinguish why an idea could work from what the paper has demonstrated. A benchmark, observation, analysis, or theory contribution may answer the question without proposing a better method. Keep the key assumptions, evaluation scope, or mixed findings needed to understand the answer.

## Organize paragraphs and contributions

A useful starting outline is four to six paragraphs:

1. **Context and task:** establish the problem and its stakes.
2. **Relevant progress:** explain the prior work needed to understand the gap.
3. **Unresolved issue:** narrow that gap to the paper's question and scope.
4. **This work:** explain the central idea, how the paper investigates it, and the main supported finding.
5. **Contribution summary, when useful:** state the distinct contributions concretely.
6. **Roadmap, when useful:** orient readers to a long or unusually organized paper, or satisfy the venue's requirement.

Merge or split these functions to fit the manuscript and venue. Give each paragraph one main point, normally expressed in its opening topic sentence; use the remaining sentences to explain, substantiate, or qualify it. Reading the topic sentences alone should reveal the argument's progression.

If using a contribution list, two to four items are a starting point. Each should identify what was established or produced and be traceable to a section, result, proof, or released resource. Keep contributions distinct; routine implementation steps do not each need a separate claim. Preview the central result with its relevant scope, leaving detailed derivations and experimental inventories to the body.

## Draft and check

Reuse the existing outline and evidence notes to map the gap, proposed answer, and contributions to their support. Missing citations or unfinished results can remain explicit editorial placeholders in a working draft; they must be resolved or the claims narrowed before submission. Use the main skill's figure-planning reference if an introductory display or its placeholder would help explain the problem or central idea.

- **Continuity:** each move motivates the next; the contribution answers the gap actually stated.
- **Fairness and support:** the closest work is represented accurately, and claimed limitations, distinctions, and results have support.
- **Consistency:** the introduction agrees with the abstract, body, and conclusion in contribution, terminology, and claim strength.
- **Focus:** each paragraph advances the argument; remove generic background, repeated motivation, and detail better placed in Related Work or the contribution section.
