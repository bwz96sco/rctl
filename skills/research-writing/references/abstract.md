# Abstract

Present a concise statement of the paper's contribution: the problem, central idea, supported answer, and significance. Select what makes that contribution understandable within the available space.

## Build the argument

For a short unstructured abstract, start with four moves, often occupying about five sentences. Treat these allocations as guidance; the venue's actual word limit and required structure govern the final form.

| Move | Starting allocation | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Context | One sentence | Identify the field and task directly, with only the background needed to understand the problem. |
| Problem | One or two sentences | State the precise constraint, trade-off, or unanswered question and why it matters. Give the scientific problem particular attention. |
| Methodology / contribution | One or two sentences | Explain the central idea and how it addresses that problem. Keep implementation details subordinate to the reasoning. |
| Results | One sentence | Give the supported answer, using concrete findings and relevant quantitative evidence where applicable. Connect its significance to the original problem. |

## Connect the problem to its evidence

Prefer a precise explanation of the unresolved problem to a vague statement that existing methods fail. Use a structural or mechanistic explanation when the analysis supports it. When only an empirical limitation is established, state that limitation accurately; a more fundamental-sounding explanation must not be invented.

Make the method's rationale visible, while keeping it distinct from demonstrated effectiveness. Choose results that answer the named problem: a robustness claim calls for evidence under relevant changes or perturbations, which overall accuracy alone may not provide.

Select the most informative result rather than listing every experiment. Preserve the metric, meaningful comparison conditions, and scope when compressing a numerical claim. Keep percentage-point changes distinct from relative changes, and retain verified counts when dataset or model coverage matters. A statistical-significance claim needs its own supporting analysis.

## Adapt to the contribution and drafting stage

The contribution slot can describe a method, benchmark, observation, analysis, or proof. A benchmark abstract should connect the evaluation gap to its design and findings; a theory abstract should state the result and relevant assumptions. Report the actual answer, including negative or mixed findings, without forcing an improvement narrative.

Read the current manuscript and retained evidence before revising the abstract. If results are unfinished, draft the supported context, problem, and contribution, and leave a clearly editorial placeholder for the missing answer. Keep that placeholder out of submission-ready text. Revisit the abstract when evidence or the paper's central claim changes; a provisional draft does not establish a completed result.

## Revise and check

- **Logical connection:** the problem, contribution, and reported result concern the same question and scope.
- **Faithfulness:** each claim and number is supported by the current paper; shortening preserves qualifiers and terminology. A wording edit does not authorize a stronger scientific claim.
- **Independent readability:** a reader can identify the task, unresolved problem, central idea, and answer without consulting a figure or section. Remove generic scene-setting, unnecessary acronyms, and inventories of implementation components.
- **Fit:** follow the requested format and actual venue limit; check the word count when a limit is specified. Deliver the requested abstract or audit under the main skill's editing and build rules.
