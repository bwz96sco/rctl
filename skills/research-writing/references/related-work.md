# Related Work

Situate the paper in the relevant research context and organize prior work into its main approaches. Explain what problems each approach has addressed and what limitations or open questions remain. Use that account to position the present contribution: how it extends, differs from, complements, or improves on prior work.

## Select and organize the relevant work

Start from the paper's question and existing literature notes. Include the closest work and select other sources for what they establish about the problem, approach, or evidence. Citation count is not a coverage goal.

Choose an organization that helps readers understand the contribution:

- **By research direction:** group the questions or lines of inquiry that the paper draws on or contributes to.
- **By method category:** when many approaches address the same problem, compare their central strategies, assumptions, demonstrated strengths, and limits.

Use descriptive headings and explain the grouping. Preserve meaningful differences within a category; shared labels do not imply identical assumptions or findings. Chronology is useful when a change over time explains the research, rather than as a default list of papers.

The introduction selects prior work to motivate the question and gap. Related Work develops the relevant research landscape and technical distinctions. If the venue or manuscript combines these functions, keep both without repeating the same motivation.

## Synthesize each group

A paragraph or short group of paragraphs can follow four moves:

1. **Name the line of work:** open with the question, strategy, or finding that connects its sources.
2. **Anchor it in representative work:** cite the papers that establish the point, including the closest work where relevant.
3. **Explain what the work established:** synthesize its achievements, common features, assumptions, and important differences.
4. **Connect it to this paper:** state a supported scope boundary, unresolved issue, or specific relationship to the present contribution.

Give an individual paper more space when its distinction matters to the argument. Avoid a sequence of disconnected summaries or a citation cluster whose members do not all support the attached claim.

Describe prior work in its intended setting. A task it did not study is not automatically a failure, and a limitation of one method does not establish a limitation of the entire category. Claims such as "all existing methods," "few studies," or "the first" require literature coverage that supports them; otherwise narrow the statement.

## State the contribution's relationship precisely

Choose the relationship supported by the paper and sources. These are useful descriptions, not a required taxonomy:

| Relationship | What to explain |
|---|---|
| Extension | What established result or approach is extended, and to which setting, task, data, or model. |
| Complement | Which different part of the problem this paper addresses, such as evaluating an existing method. |
| Contrast | Which assumption, task definition, approach, or evaluation objective differs, and why that difference matters. |
| Unification | Which previously separate problems or methods the paper connects, and through what common account. |
| Re-evaluation | What is reassessed, which omission or bias the new evaluation examines, and what the evidence establishes. |

Explain the closest work's similarities and the specific distinction together. A difference alone does not demonstrate novelty or superiority. If claiming an improvement, identify the relevant comparison, conditions, and supporting evidence. Keep neutral findings, trade-offs, and unresolved comparisons visible where they affect the positioning.

## Use a comparison table when it clarifies the map

A table can make several works comparable along a small set of meaningful dimensions. Select dimensions that are recognized or well justified in the field and directly relevant to the paper's core question. A benchmark paper might compare subject coverage and data types; a method paper might compare assumptions, required information, and evaluation settings.

Apply the same definitions to every row and retain the sources for the entries. Do not construct a checklist merely because the present work is the only row that satisfies it. Distinguish an unreported or unverified property from an absent one, and a listed feature from demonstrated effectiveness.

Use the [figure-planning reference](figure-planning.md) for the table's placement, caption, text relationship, and placeholder. The prose should explain the consequential distinctions rather than repeat every cell.

## Draft and check

Reuse the literature notes and manuscript outline; no separate comparison document is required. Mark missing source support visibly in a working draft, then verify it or narrow the statement before submission.

- **Coverage:** the closest work is included, and every group helps explain the paper's question or contribution.
- **Synthesis:** paragraphs convey a shared idea and meaningful differences, with citations supporting the precise claims attached to them.
- **Fairness:** achievements and limits retain their original scope; comparisons use consistent, relevant criteria.
- **Positioning:** the relationship is concrete and agrees with the introduction, contribution, and results, without repeating their content unnecessarily.
